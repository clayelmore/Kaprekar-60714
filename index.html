A Generalized Kaprekar Phenomenon
60714

A five-digit number reached by every input at 5 and 6 digits; at 7 or more digits every input reaches it or collapses to 0, and the share collapsing to 0 grows overall with length, though not at every step.

Clay Elmore November 2026

Kaprekar's classical process fixes 6174 at four digits and 495 at three. The classical rule, applied at d = 5, falls into one of three cycles and has no fixed endpoint.

The paper allows any rule that sorts the digits and subtracts one rearrangement from another. At d = 5, exhaustive enumeration over all 5,280 full-variable rules yields 33 universal fixed points; at d = 6, 506. Of the 33 at d = 5, exactly one, 60714, is universal again at d = 6 (counting only rules in which every digit takes part, at both lengths; if a letter may stay in place and only repeated-digit inputs are excluded, there are 54, of which 22 recur).

Computed: at d = 5, 6 every input that is not one repeated digit reaches 60714 (at five digits: 99,990 of 100,000 strings, in at most 30 steps). This is a complete computer check, not a hand proof.

Shown in the paper (not refereed): 60714 is a fixed point of an explicit rule at every d ≥ 5, and at d ≥ 7 every input reaches 60714 or 0. The inputs that reach 0 form an exactly characterized escape class, and its share grows overall with length, though not at every step: 0.71% of digit collections at 7 digits, 0.56% at 8, about 12% at 24 (computed). The paper's argument runs by induction along two ladders (odd and even d), algebraic at large d and resting on stated complete enumerations at small d. The paper also studies the repetition direction, the digits of 6174 written m times; there the question for every m is open.

Main Result

Theorem 4.1. The integer 60714 is a fixed point of an explicit coefficient-preserving lifting K(d) at every digit length d ≥ 5. At d = 5, 6 every admissible input reaches 60714. At d ≥ 7 every admissible input outside an exactly characterized escape class Ed reaches 60714, and every input in Ed reaches 0.

The inputs that the rule sends to 0 in one step number C(⌊(d−3)/2⌋ + 9, 9) − 10. The whole escape class is larger: the share of inputs that collapse to 0 grows overall with length, though not at every step, from 0.56% of digit collections at 8 digits to about 12% at 24 digits (computed, November 2026).

The Rule at d = 5

Sort the digits of the input in descending order as (a, b, c, d, e). Form two new five-digit numbers by interleaving the digits in fixed patterns, then subtract:

K(n) = | adcbe − deacb |

Both adcbe and deacb are five-digit integers built by reading a, b, c, d, e in the indicated order. The classical Kaprekar process at four digits uses descending minus ascending, abcd − dcba; that simple recipe has no full-variable fixed point at five digits. The interleaved rule adcbe − deacb fixes 60714, and every five-digit input that is not one repeated digit reaches 60714 under it.

Applied to 60714 itself: digits sorted descending are (7, 6, 4, 1, 0), so adcbe = 71460 and deacb = 10746, and 71460 − 10746 = 60714. ✓

Worked Example

Starting from 10135, the rule reaches 60714 in two iterations.

Step 1
Input: 10135

Sort digits descending and label them:

5a
3b
1c
1d
0e
5 1 1 3 0 = adcbe
− 1 0 5 1 3 = deacb
4 0 6 1 7 = 40617
↓
Step 2
Input: 40617

Sort digits descending and label them:

7a
6b
4c
1d
0e
7 1 4 6 0 = adcbe
− 1 0 7 4 6 = deacb
6 0 7 1 4 = 60714
→ reached 60714 ✓

Notice that the intermediate value 40617 has the same digit multiset as 60714, just permuted. Every five-digit number whose digits are {0, 1, 4, 6, 7} sorts to (7, 6, 4, 1, 0), and the rule maps that descending arrangement directly to 60714. So the second step is the universal "snap to fixed point" move, while the first step is what gets the digit multiset there in the first place.
